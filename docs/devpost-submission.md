## Project name

Chip Autopsy Lab

## Tagline

Drop an STDF. Get the cause of death.

## Category

Frankenstein. Chip Autopsy Lab stitches four parts that were never meant to share a body:

- The bones: STDF V4, a 1990s binary log format from ATE testers, parsed byte by byte inside a browser Web Worker.
- The eyes: WM-811K, a public research dataset of 811,457 real fab wafer maps, turned into a 9-class spatial classifier.
- The voice: an optional LLM narrator on a short leash. It sees aggregate facts only, and the client rejects any sentence that cites a fact the engine didn't produce.
- The skin: a modern Next.js and Canvas2D interface in 5 languages, set as a 3D forensic bench.

Test-floor data, academic research, generative AI, and the web rarely meet. Stitched together, they turn a raw tester log into a cause of death you can click through to the dies that prove it, with no install.

## Inspiration

I'm an IC design undergraduate training as an ATE test engineer. The users are ATE, test, and yield engineers at small fabless teams and OSATs, plus students learning wafer sort. When a lot comes back with bad yield, opening its STDF binary means a licensed enterprise yield tool or a custom Python script, then Excel and matplotlib for every wafer. The free browser viewers draw wafer maps and Pareto charts but leave the diagnosis to you. Chip Autopsy Lab reads the raw STDF log in the browser and opens with the cause of death, with the dies that prove it one click away.

## What it does

You drop an STDF V4 file (plain or gzip) into the browser, or open one of three built-in demo lots. The app opens with one sentence, such as "Cause of death: Edge-Ring failure", followed by the yield and the bin that lost the most dies.

- Every claim links to evidence. Clicking the verdict or a finding lights up its dies on the wafer map and its bar in the bin Pareto.
- Wafer maps in Pass/Fail, hard bin, soft bin, and parametric modes, with a hover magnifier, zoom, and pan.
- A stacked heatmap across all wafers in a lot shows hot spots that come back wafer after wafer.
- Yield by lot, wafer, and test site, with first-pass yield, final yield, retest counts, and single-site anomaly detection.
- Parametric histograms with test limits, plus mean, sigma, `Cp`, and `Cpk`.
- A classifier for the 9 WM-811K spatial classes, with confidence and possible causes.
- Brushing a region of the wafer filters the Pareto, yield, and parametric views live.
- A `Cmd-K` palette understands queries like "show edge failures with Cpk < 1.33" with an offline grammar.
- An agent console at `/agents` that runs a published four-agent network over the same engine, on a demo router when no key is set, with every claim tied to a fact id.
- 5 UI languages, light and dark themes, keyboard navigation, and reduced-motion support.

The interface is Carbon, the third design pass. The landing page sets the sample lot on a tilted 3D deck over a telemetry strip; opening a lot unboxes the verdict next to the wafer map; an Evidence shelf opens into the Pareto, yield, wafer-gallery and parametric charts without leaving the screen. Every screen switch runs as a view transition, and on a phone the same surfaces answer to tap, press and scroll instead of hover.

Parsing and analysis run in a Web Worker on your machine. Raw STDF bytes never leave the browser. The optional LLM narrator receives aggregate facts only, and the client rejects any narration that cites a fact the engine didn't produce.

## How we built it

I wrote the spec first in Kiro: EARS requirements, a design with 14 correctness properties, and a task list. Then I built the core bottom-up. `src/core/` is framework-free TypeScript: an STDF V4 parser and writer for 16 record types, a columnar lot model with retest resolution, analytics, the WM-811K classifier, a report engine, and a filter DSL. ESLint blocks React, Next.js, and DOM imports there. The UI is Next.js, React, and Tailwind v4 over a custom layered Canvas2D wafer renderer, with visx charts, Zustand for one shared selection store, and Comlink to the worker. The demo lots are generated by my own STDF writer and parsed by the same pipeline as a dropped file.

The interface took the longest. Earlier passes were clean, then clean with motion, and I rejected both as flat. Carbon, the current design language, is built on a CSS 3D spatial system: preserve-3d tilt plates, a landing deck that carries the sample lot, an evidence shelf that opens in place, view transitions between screens, and a touch motion layer that answers to tap, press and scroll when there is no hover. The commit subjects record who wrote what, and this is the honest split: the Carbon interface steps came from `agy Gemini 4 Carbon` (`59a879e`, `da64e4c`, `c9b4866`, `a82dce9`, `fafff5b`, `d9fce1f`), Kiro reviewed and fixed that work (`0f5bf0a`), and Kiro wrote the view transitions (`0bf931e`) and the touch and scroll motion (`87bf4d4`) itself with `claude-opus-5.5`. The spec, the steering, the hooks, the task list and the verification of every task stayed in Kiro.

## Challenges we ran into

- STDF V4 is unforgiving: optional trailing fields, `PTR` defaults inherited from the first record of a test, multi-site interleaving, and retests. The fix was one shared record table for the parser and the writer, so round-trip property tests cover every field.
- My first rule-based classifier scored 51.3% accuracy on the real WM-811K test split. I added handcrafted features and a small logistic regression trained on the train split only, and ran the test split once after freezing the model.
- Keeping motion smooth while the wafer redraws took a perf gate that measures every frame in Playwright. The 3D bench made it harder again: a tilted deck, SVG pulse bridges and an oscilloscope all move over the same canvas, so the motion stays on transform and opacity, and phones get their own tap-and-scroll layer because a hover tilt never fires there.
- In the last polish round, my own fixes looked like they did nothing. Kiro found why: Playwright was serving the old production build, so the e2e suite tested stale code (`logs/redesign-R8-kiro.log`).

## Accomplishments that we're proud of

- 97.8% accuracy and macro-F1 0.885 on 51,886 held-out real WM-811K wafers.
- 555 unit and property tests in 92 files, including 16 `fast-check` suites that cover all 14 spec properties, plus 54 Playwright spec files.
- Automated gates for privacy (no third-party requests, no STDF bytes in any request body) and for secrets in the client bundle.
- A judge can open the live demo and reach a linked verdict with no install and no API key.
- A real third-party STDF log opened with about 1.7 s from drop to verdict: 78.3% yield, `SBIN 4` (`DELAY`) as the bin that costs the most, and `Delay Line #07` the weakest test at `Cpk` 0.48. Measured on the deployed site; the log is not redistributed.

## What we learned

A spec with written correctness properties made the agent's work checkable. When a property failed, it pointed at the bug, not at the test. I also learned that WM-811K labels are noisy, so macro-F1 and per-class recall matter more than headline accuracy, because 85% of wafers are `None`. And a design is not finished when it is clean: the earlier interface passes were clean and forgettable, and the pass that worked was the one that gave the bench depth, motion and a point of view.

## What's next for Chip Autopsy Lab

- Better recall on faint `Loc`, `Edge-Loc`, and `Scratch` patterns, which are the main misses today.
- Re-run the perf gate on real hardware. The sandbox numbers have software-raster caveats.
- Test with real STDF logs from engineers at small fabless teams and OSATs.

## How Kiro was used

### From spec to verified code

Kiro's agent ran the build loop from the IDE. Every step left a file in this repo.

1. **Spec.** The spec has 17 EARS requirements, a design with 14 correctness properties, and a task list where every task cites its requirement ids. Kiro sent the first requirements and design drafts to Gemini through the Carbon MCP tool (`logs/phase2.log`, commits `af42af8`, `9076b54`), then reviewed them itself: one pass made 48 edits to `requirements.md` (`logs/kiro-review-req.log`), and another wrote `tasks.md` (`logs/kiro-tasks.log`, commit `e8af9c1`).
2. **Steering.** Four always-included files hold the product scope, the locked stack and STDF V4 decoding rules, the repo layout, and the design tokens. A fifth, `submission.md`, loads only when submission copy is edited.
3. **Hooks.** Saving a file under `src/` runs lint and typecheck. Saving under `src/core/` runs that file's unit and property tests. Saving an i18n file checks key parity across all 5 locales. Editing a spec `design.md` reruns every property suite. One manual hook rebuilds the table below from `.kiro/`.
4. **Agent tasks.** Tasks ran one at a time. Each prompt is in `logs/prompt-*.txt`, each run log is in `logs/`, and each result is in `logs/progress.md`. In `git log`, 63 commit subjects end in `(Kiro <model>)`, and 48 are tagged `(Carbon)`, meaning Kiro handed that task to Gemini through the Carbon MCP server and checked the result.
5. **Verification.** A task is ticked only after lint, typecheck, `pnpm test`, and its Playwright spec pass. This round ran the suite on `51cc080`: 555 tests in 92 files, all passing, and ESLint clean.

One trace you can follow:

- **Requirement 1.5** (`requirements.md`): check that the first record is `FAR` with `STDF_VER = 4`, then read numbers big-endian when `FAR.CPU_TYPE = 1` and little-endian when it is `2`.
- **Task 2.2** (`tasks.md`) builds `src/core/stdf/stdf-parser.ts` and cites Requirement 1.5. Sub-task 2.4 adds the property test.
- **Property 1** (`design.md`, "Validates: Requirements 1.3, 1.5, ...") says any generated lot, written in either byte order, parses back to an equal lot model. `src/core/stdf/stdf-roundtrip.prop.test.ts` checks it with `fast-check`.
- **Hooks.** `core-unit-tests-on-save.kiro.hook` runs that suite whenever a file in `src/core/stdf/` is saved, and it is told never to weaken a design property to make a test pass. `spec-properties-on-design-edit.kiro.hook` runs it again whenever `design.md` changes.

### Where Kiro surprised us

- **It found that our spec broke its own rules.** Reviewing `requirements.md`, Kiro reported: "the spec said it must be a single sentence, but its own example is two sentences." In the same pass it caught a privacy leak in the design: "the URL stored the Wafer ID, a string from the STDF file, which the privacy rule forbids. It now stores a wafer index." (`logs/kiro-review-req.log`)
- **It explained why our fixes seemed to change nothing.** During redesign task R8, Kiro found: "Playwright serves the existing production build from `.next` and rebuilds only if that build is missing. Until you run `pnpm build`, `pnpm e2e` tests old code." It then traced the remaining slow frame to the wafer-strip ArrowRight handler: "It spends 31 ms in script and 8 ms forcing a layout". (`logs/redesign-R8-kiro.log`)
- **It told us what it had not checked.** After writing the first three hooks, Kiro said: "I haven't watched them fire in Kiro, and I wrote the hook format from memory without checking it against the Kiro docs." It also noted that the parity test covered `ja` and `ko` while the hook named only the two Chinese tables. This round fixed that hook. (`logs/t22-hooks.log`)
